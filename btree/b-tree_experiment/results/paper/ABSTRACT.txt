Bit flips in DRAM silently corrupt in-memory B-tree indexes, and the damage done to a corrupted routing node can misdirect or contaminate the entire subtree beneath it. Current practice protects every page uniformly, overpaying where it matters least and leaving gaps where it matters most.

Using a fault-injection simulator over a B-tree, DRAM cache and SSD stack, we quantify the case for differential protection across more than 1,500 seed-matched runs. Without protection, each bit flip leaves 21.9 keys wrong (against a ceiling of 1 if damage stayed local) and protecting the control plane alone removes 98.7% of it, attributing the amplification to routing rather than payload corruption. The reliable-memory budget has a closed-form optimum equal to the storage of the internal nodes, invariant across a 123x range of internal-node counts. Finally, the tree's key-ordering invariant is a free detector whose strength is a closed form in key sparsity, but internal separators are sparse by construction and thus carry a structural blind spot on exactly the pages every traversal reads.

Protection should therefore be allocated by level and field role, with parameters computable before deployment from fanout and key distribution.
