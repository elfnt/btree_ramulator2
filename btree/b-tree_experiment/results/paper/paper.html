Where Bit Flips Hurt: Per-Level Differential Protection for In-Memory B+-Trees

Author — Institution

Abstract

Bit flips in DRAM silently corrupt in-memory B+-tree indexes, and a corrupted routing node can misdirect or contaminate the entire subtree beneath it. Current practice protects every page uniformly, overpaying where it matters least and leaving gaps where it matters most. Using a fault-injection simulator over a B+-tree, DRAM cache and SSD stack, we quantify the case for differential protection across 1,407 seed-matched runs.1 Without protection, each bit flip leaves 21.9 keys wrong (against a ceiling of 1 if damage stayed local), and a checksum over the control plane alone (node header, keys, child pointers) removes 98.7 % of that damage and 99.4 % of silent wrong answers at 40 % of the latency cost of a full-page checksum. The amplification is therefore a routing effect, not payload corruption. The reliable-memory budget has a closed-form optimum equal to the storage of the internal nodes, invariant across a 122× range of internal-node counts. Finally, the tree's key-ordering invariant is a free detector whose strength is a closed form in key sparsity, (64−k)/64, but internal separators are sparse by construction and so carry a structural blind spot on exactly the pages every traversal reads. Padding the key space with dummy keys buys about one bit of coverage per doubling of the tree; changing the key representation is far cheaper. Protection should therefore be allocated by level and field role, with parameters computable before deployment from fanout and key distribution.

1Introduction

An in-memory B+-tree is a small routing structure on top of a large payload: the few internal nodes are read on every lookup, each leaf only when its keys are wanted. A DRAM bit flip in a leaf value damages one key; the same flip in an internal separator or child pointer misroutes every later lookup through it, and inserts that follow the wrong route land in the wrong subtree (Figure 1). Uniform page protection, such as a CRC over every page, pays the same verification cost for both.

We ask how much protection each part of the tree deserves, and whether the answer can be computed in advance. Four schemes (none, a free structural check, a control-plane checksum, a full-page checksum) are compared across eight experiments. Contributions:

  • Attribution. The control plane is about 35–38 % of corruption events but carries almost all wrong answers. CTRL_CRC, a CRC over header, keys and child pointers, cuts the silent-corruption (SDC) rate by 99.4 % at 40 % of the latency overhead of FULL_CRC and the same reliable memory (§4.1).
  • A closed-form budget. With a level-aware eviction policy, SDC saturates at exactly the bytes needed to cover every internal node, Bint, in all four tree shapes we test (131 to 16,076 internal nodes) (§4.2).
  • Robustness. Uniform faults are the worst case at scale (§4.3); periodic verification saves little once only the control plane is checked (§4.4); shrinking DRAM concentrates the resident set on internal nodes, so eviction is not free scrubbing (§4.5); the CTRL_CRC/FULL_CRC cost ratio follows the read-weighted control-plane length (§4.6).
  • Key sparsity. The ordering check detects a fraction (64−k)/64 of what a checksum detects when keys are 2k apart, and its blind zone follows the internal separator gap, so it is blind on the routing pages even for dense keys (§4.7). Grid dummy keys raise the ratio by about one bit per doubling of pages while end-to-end SDC worsens; adjacency, shifting and hashing are the useful alternatives (§4.8).

2Protection schemes and the reliable-memory model

Page layout. A node occupies one 4,096-byte page: bytes [0, 16) hold an SSD header, [16, 32) the node header (type, n_keys, leaf link), then keys[] (8 B each), then either children[] (internal nodes) or values[] (leaves); the rest is zero padding. The control plane is ctrl_len = 16 + 8n bytes for a leaf with n keys and 24 + 16n bytes for an internal node, which is the whole live content of an internal node. At order 253 a typical leaf holds about 174 keys, so its control plane is about 1,408 B, its values 1,392 B and its unused slack about 1,280 B.

Schemes. Table 1 lists the four schemes. A header-only CRC covers 16 of the 4,080 mutable bytes (under 1 % of a dense page); in our runs it left the SDC rate unchanged from no protection, which motivates extending the checksum to the full control plane (CTRL_CRC). SANITY needs no stored state: it is the set of invariants a B+-tree node must satisfy anyway.

Reliable memory and the shadow store. Checksums live in a small reliable memory (e.g. ECC-protected or on-chip SRAM, 20 ns per access) organised as a shadow store of 12-byte entries keyed by page. Its capacity is a budget B in bytes. A page without an entry is read unverified. When the store is full, an eviction policy decides which pages keep entries: BY_LEVEL admits a page only if its level is above the lowest level present and then evicts the oldest entry of the lowest level; LRU evicts the least recently used entry; BY_HOTNESS evicts the least frequently accessed (count without ageing); RANDOM evicts uniformly at random. We write Bint = ninternal × 12 B for the bytes needed to cover every internal node and Bfull = pages × 12 B for full coverage.

3Experimental method

Simulator stack. A B+-tree of order 253 (the largest that fits a 4,096-byte internal node: 32 + 253 × 8 + 254 × 8 = 4,088 B) stores 8-byte keys and values in pages cached in a simulated DRAM (fixed 100 ns access, capacity 8 × rows-per-bank pages) in front of an SSD (100 µs, write-through). Each node read passes a guarded-read layer that applies the scheme and, for scoring only, compares the page with an oracle image at zero cost.

Fault injection. The default model flips uniformly random bits in resident DRAM pages as a Poisson process of rate λ flips per page per second (default λ = 3×10−3, deliberately accelerated). §4.3 adds row hammer (0.7 events/s, each flipping bits with probability 10−4 on two adjacent rows) and one-shot column faults at t = 150 s that flip the same (byte, bit) on 512 rows. The fault stream depends only on the seed, not on the scheme.

Workload. 1,000,000 dense integer keys, inserted in shuffled order, build a tree of about 5,763 pages (h = 3, 33 internal nodes). Then 30,000 operations (10 ms simulated each) run: 90 % searches, 5 % of them for absent keys, and 10 % ascending inserts, i.e. about 27,000 searches per run. The 16,384-page DRAM keeps the tree resident except in §4.5.

Metrics. SDC rate = undetected wrong answers per search, scored against a reference map. Cost = reliable memory (peak shadow-store bytes) and pages allocated; time = mean operation latency, with overhead relative to NONE at the same setting. We do not use recall: for SANITY and CTRL_CRC it is dominated by harmless misses on payload and slack bytes.

Scale, seeds and pairing. The 1 M-key, order-253 scale was chosen after smoke tests (worst run about 50 s). E1 and E5–E7 use 5 seeds, E2–E4 use 3 (Appendix A). The fault schedule depends only on the seed, so schemes are compared paired by seed; flip counts match exactly in 33 of 35 E1 cells (the other two differ by ≤ 0.04 %). Tables give mean ± sample SD over seeds; we report no bootstrap intervals in this draft and flag overlapping SDs in the text.

Ramulator2 replication. We re-ran E1 with a cycle-accurate DDR4-2400 backend (Ramulator2, ECC off; 4 schemes × 7 λ × 3 seeds = 84 runs). It picks flipped bits with its own generator, so it is an independent replicate. Every qualitative result holds: FULL_CRC has SDC = 0 in all runs and CTRL_CRC's SDC is within −17 % to +12 % of the main backend. Latency differs by only 0.2–0.4 % because SSD write-through dominates it (10 % inserts × 100 µs ≈ 10 µs per op). NONE differs by up to 440× at λ = 10−4, which is the heavy tail of §5, not a backend effect.

Table 3 explains the leverage. Only 35–38 % of corruption events at λ ≤ 3×10−3 touch the control plane, about a third hit live values that CTRL_CRC by design cannot see, and about 27 % hit padding that is never read. Yet the control-plane events produce nearly all wrong answers, because a damaged separator or child pointer is re-read and misroutes on every traversal, while a damaged value affects one key.

Cost. CTRL_CRC and FULL_CRC hold the same reliable memory (69,154 B, 12 B × pages), so the difference is all in time. At the verification floor (λ = 10−5, almost no recoveries) CTRL_CRC adds +25.3 % and FULL_CRC +61.7 %, a ratio of 41 %; at the working point the ratio is 40 % (7.20 vs 18.06 µs).4 Latency grows with λ for every detecting scheme because each detection triggers a 100 µs SSD reload.

SANITY. With dense keys, SANITY's SDC is at most 26 % above CTRL_CRC's up to λ = 10−2, at lower latency (+44 % vs +69 %). At λ = 3×10−2 it degrades to 1.01e-2 ± 8.8e-3, against CTRL_CRC's 2.32e-3 ± 2.4e-4. §4.7 shows that the near-tie is a property of dense keys.

Findings. Per effective flip, uniform faults cause 0.693 undetected wrong answers under NONE, hammer 0.247 and column faults 0.011–0.016, so uniform is about 43× worse than a column fault. A column fault hits 512 rows, which is 8.9 % of a 5,767-page tree, and each damaged key byte affects one of about 174 keys in its leaf; uniform flips spread over every page and so meet far more lookups. This reverses the result at toy scale (order 8, 1,339 pages), where the same column fault reached 38 % of the tree.

The two column faults isolate CTRL_CRC's boundary. At order 253, byte 128 lies in a leaf's keys[]: CTRL_CRC detects all 512 flips and has SDC = 0, matching FULL_CRC at +39.7 % latency instead of +75.9 %. Byte 3000 lies in values[]: CTRL_CRC sees almost nothing and lowers SDC only 53 %, and SANITY is identical to NONE because values carry no invariant. This is the intended trade-off, not a defect; §5 discusses protecting values where they matter.

End to end it gets worse. SANITY's SDC goes from 1.06e-3 with no dummies to 1.41e-3, 1.36e-3 and 1.22e-3 (undetected answers per run 28.6 → 38.0, 36.8, 33.0; missing answers 10.2 → 14.8, 16.8, 14.0). More pages receive more flips (5,195 → 41,318 per run at 8×) and hold more internal nodes in the blind zone; the 8× tree is also taller (h = 4), a confound. With n = 5 the differences are within seed spread, but no grid arm beats the baseline. Cost scales with pages: 2× / 4× / 8× pages give 1.89× / 3.57× / 6.83× SANITY latency, and CTRL_CRC's reliable memory grows 69,154 → 552,943 B.

Contrasts. Adjacent dummies make every key's low bits matter to its neighbour: ratio 0.976 at 2.00× pages and 2.01× latency, with SDC 1.10e-3 (about the baseline; missing answers fall to 7.2). Shift adds no pages or reliable memory and yields exactly the stride-0 tree, so SANITY behaves as at k = 0 (ratio 0.981, SDC 7.18e-4, −32 % vs the stride-232 baseline); its 1.17× latency is the cost of reloading the extra detected pages. It needs a known stride. For irregular keys, hash mapping lowers SANITY's SDC from 1.24e-3 (unmapped random keys, ratio 0.298) to 7.92e-4–9.62e-4 at about 2× pages and 2.31–2.41× latency (1.17–2.50 probes per hit), but gives up key order and hence range scans. E21 has no CTRL_CRC rows, so no ratio is reported.

Verdict. Grid dummies buy about one bit of SANITY coverage per doubling of the tree, which is the predicted rate and not worth paying. Where keys are sparse, change the representation (shift, or hash mapping when order is not needed) or use adjacency; otherwise checksum the control plane.

5Discussion

What follows for differential protection. The results reduce protection to quantities known before deployment. (1) Where to spend reliable memory: on internal nodes first, with a level-aware policy and a budget of Bint = ninternal × entry size, which follows from N and the fanout. (2) What to checksum: the control plane, whose cost relative to a full-page checksum is the read-weighted ctrl_len / 4,096, about 0.03 for sparse nodes and 0.35–0.45 at order 253. (3) When a free check suffices: the ordering invariant covers (64−k)/64 of key flips, nearly a checksum on dense leaves, but its separator blind zone leaves internal nodes needing a real checksum. Values stay unprotected under CTRL_CRC (about a third of events at order 253); a checksum on hot leaves is the natural next step, not evaluated here.

Limitations. Accelerated λ. λ = 3×10−3 flips/page/s is far above field DRAM rates; the 3.5-decade sweep shows the ranking is stable, but absolute SDC rates are not field predictions. DRAM-only flips. We inject only into DRAM-resident pages: no SSD, controller, CPU-cache or reliable-memory faults, and ECC is off. Single workload. 90 % point searches and 10 % ascending inserts on integer keys, single-threaded, with no deletes and no range scans; latency comes from fixed constants and is dominated by SSD write-through. Frozen-clock scan. The end-of-run integrity scan does not advance the clock, degrading the LRU during the scan; we omit ram_misses in E5 (SDC, latency and flips are unaffected). NONE heavy tail. Unprotected SDC depends on whether a flip hits a busy level-1 node (SD ≈ 0.73 × mean at 1 M keys, 5 seeds); read NONE magnitudes as orders of magnitude. E3 confounds. Fault modes differ in flip count and timing as well as structure (column faults are one-shot at t = 150 s, 512 flips against about 4,470), so per-flip normalisation compares mechanisms only approximately. E2–E4 use 3 seeds and the original CTRL_CRC variant, and no bootstrap intervals are reported.

6Conclusion

In a dense in-memory B+-tree, silent corruption is a routing problem. A checksum over the control plane removes about 99 % of silent wrong answers at roughly 40 % of the time cost of checksumming whole pages, and a reliable-memory budget equal to the internal nodes' storage captures nearly all of the benefit, in every tree shape we tried. The free ordering check is a useful complement whose strength is a closed form in key sparsity. Its blind spot on separators, and the poor return from padding keys with dummies, argue for checksumming internal nodes and fixing key representation instead. Each parameter (Bint, the cost ratio, the ordering-check coverage) can be computed from fanout and key distribution before deployment.
